AI Agents Got Cheap and Capable. Here's What to Build

A month of AI news in plain English: cheaper model tiers, agents doing real work, the safety holes to close, and where small builders should look next.

Key takeaways
  • AI keeps getting cheaper, and the gap between small and big models keeps growing. Send each task to the cheapest model that does it well.
  • Agents now do real work. At one lab, models write over 80% of the code.
  • Agents also break rules. One got out of a no-internet sandbox through a gap nobody watched. Lock down every agent you build, not just the obvious parts.
  • Cheap boards like a Raspberry Pi plus AI turn "check it every few months" into "watch it all the time". That's a business opening for small builders.
  • Read AI news with care. Many numbers come from the company's own tests.

"Startups & Ventures" (@tech) is a Telegram tech-news channel with about 17,000 subscribers. It's run by the same ad network as the markets channel in my Follow the AI Money post. I read about 55 posts from mid-September to mid-October 2026.

Despite the name, it has almost nothing on startups or venture capital. It's short tech news, mostly about AI. I cut the films, games, gadgets and politics and kept the one thread that matters if you build things: AI agents, meaning AI that takes actions rather than only chatting. Figures are as the channel reported them, often from the company's own tests. Each one links to its post so you can check it. The plain-English explanations and section 7 are mine.

1. AI keeps getting cheaper, and the tiers spread out

AI companies charge per token, roughly three-quarters of a word. In one month the channel reported price cuts from almost every big lab:

ModelWhat changedGood for
Claude Opus 5.540% cheaper than the last version: $4 per million input tokens, $20 output, and $0.20 for repeated input (post)Long coding and agent tasks
New Claude HaikuUp to 20× cheaper than the mid-size model (post)Summaries, documents, routine work
GPT-6 Sol and LunaHalf the price of the previous series (post)Sol for coding; Luna for summaries and pulling out data
Microsoft Decision-1Doesn't write text. It scores a set of options in one go, at $0.042 per million input tokens (post)Sorting, classifying, deciding "continue or ask a human"

Notice the spread. The cheapest options cost a tiny fraction of the biggest ones. So the question isn't "which AI is best?" It's "what's the cheapest model that does this particular job well?" That's called routing.

Example: sorting 10,000 reviews

Xbox used Decision-1 to sort more than 10,000 player reviews. Microsoft says it was 14× faster and 200× cheaper than a big general model (post). That's their own claim, but the point stands: choosing between labels doesn't need a model that writes essays.

Routing is moving into the operating system too. Microsoft is rebuilding Windows so each task is sent to a model on your laptop or one in the cloud, whichever fits (post).

2. Agents now do real work

A paper by 22 researchers, including Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio and people from OpenAI and Anthropic, put numbers on how fast this is moving (post):

The authors think full automation of AI research could make progress about 10× faster within roughly a year and a half. Computing power or data could slow that down. They suggest labs publicly report how much of their research is automated, and get outside audits before releasing models.

Real examples from the same month:

  • Big code moves in a day. A tester moved a 680,000-line codebase to new tech in under a day with Claude Opus 5.5. Another reviewed and fixed 200,000 lines in 3 hours, against 20+ hours with the previous model (post).
  • Agents that use your computer. Windows is being rebuilt so agents can work with your files and apps, inside walled-off containers (post).
  • Agents find the shortcut. GPT-6 Astra played through a starting area of World of Warcraft by reading the game's data feed instead of looking at the screen. It also wrote its own route planner (post).
  • Always-on agents are next. Leaks point to an OpenAI agent that keeps working outside a chat and may get its own email address (post).

3. Agents also break out, so lock them down

This is the most useful story in the whole channel, because it's a mistake anyone building agents could make.

Example: the DNS escape

On 20 September, an OpenAI agent was running in a sandbox with no internet access. It still managed to send questions to an outside chatbot by hiding them inside DNS lookups, the system computers use to turn names like google.com into addresses. Web traffic was blocked, but nobody had blocked DNS. OpenAI paused training its most powerful models until the gap was closed (post).

It isn't a one-off. Axios reported "tens of thousands" of incidents under investigation at OpenAI and Anthropic, including agents getting around their rules. Anthropic's own report says Opus 5.5 tried to escape a test sandbox in 1.5% of runs (post). Across hundreds of thousands of runs, 1.5% is a lot.

The fixes the big companies are shipping make a good checklist for any agent you build for a client:

  • Give access file by file. Windows' new agent containers let you grant access to selected files, or to the internet, separately (post).
  • Close every way out, not only the web. DNS, email and any other connection count too.
  • Check before acting. Claude agents now face an action check before they run something (post).
  • Know when to hand over. A small, cheap model like Decision-1 can decide when to stop and ask a person (post).
The bigger risk is people

Security experts quoted by the channel worry less about rogue agents than about AI helping unskilled attackers. A model can read old equipment manuals and walk someone through attacking it, and the average US nuclear reactor is 44 years old (post).

4. The best answer may not be a chat

Andrej Karpathy, a well-known AI researcher, suggested two habits (post):

  • Ask AI to explain hard topics in simplified technical English (a standard called ASD-STE100): short sentences, one meaning per word.
  • For harder subjects, ask for a diagram or a one-off web page instead of paragraphs. Now that code is cheap, a small custom tool for one question is practical.

His bigger point: the next AI interface may be an answer built for each task, not an endless chat full of text. Phones are already trying it. The Xiaomi 18 Pro runs small AI-made apps, like an egg timer or a water reminder, on a second screen on its back (post).

It's also why posts on this blog lean on diagrams: a picture often explains in five seconds what takes five paragraphs.

5. Cheap hardware turns checking into watching

One small post holds a big idea. Someone built a railway-track monitor on a Raspberry Pi, a credit-card-sized computer that costs a few thousand rupees. It has a camera, AI that spots cracks, GPS and a vibration sensor, and it logs everything automatically (post). The aim is to replace inspections every few months with continuous watching.

The same pattern fits plenty of places: machines in a small factory, cold storage, solar panels, a warehouse. Meta has released an open kit that connects its AI agent to cheap boards like the Raspberry Pi and ESP32, and to lights, TVs and speakers (post). The parts are cheap now. The work is in setting it up and knowing what to watch for.

Example: robots that teach themselves

Skild AI trained a humanoid robot to play football by letting it play against earlier versions of itself for 140 simulated years. Its only reward was scoring goals. It learned dribbling, passing and defending without being taught any of them (post). The method works anywhere there's a clear goal and a good simulator.

A reality check from the same month: researchers built wallpaper that makes electricity from humidity. It produces microwatts, enough for a wireless keyboard or a small sensor, but nowhere near a fridge (post). Clever doesn't mean useful at scale. Check the numbers.

6. Ripple effects to plan around

  • Cheap phones get scarcer. AI demand is pushing up chip prices, and phones under $200 could ship 40% less by 2030 (post). If your customers use budget phones, keep your apps light.
  • Ads are coming to AI tools. ChatGPT will show labelled image ads in its image generator in the US. Paid plans stay ad-free, and there are no ads around health topics (post).
  • Proof that a photo is real. The iPhone 18 Pro signs each photo inside the camera sensor, so any edit fails the check (post). With fakes everywhere, proof becomes a feature.
  • Privacy built in. Apple is reportedly building a home camera that sends only text ("someone entered the room") and keeps no video (post). No footage means nothing to leak, but also nothing to review later.

7. What I'd do with this

This is my own read, for founders and freelancers building AI for small businesses.

  1. Route by tier from day one. Start every task on the smallest model and move up only when it clearly fails. Keep a note of which tasks needed the big one.
  2. Build the simplest version first. A rule I picked up building an AI data pipeline: an agent has to beat a plain step-by-step script on both coverage and errors before it ships. Often the script wins.
  3. Lock down every agent for clients with the four layers in section 3. Write down which files it can touch, which connections are open, and which actions need a person.
  4. Look for "checked every few months" problems. Anywhere a business inspects something on a schedule, a cheap sensor plus a small model could watch it all the time.
  5. Explain with pictures. Clients understand a one-page diagram faster than a long proposal. Karpathy's point applies to sales too.

8. How to read AI news without being misled

  • "In internal tests." When a company tests its own model, it picks the test. "35× faster than GPT-6 Sol in internal tests" (post) is a claim, not a result.
  • Benchmarks the company chose. A model that "beat" a rival on two coding tests (post) may lose on the five tests that weren't mentioned.
  • Leaks and codenames. Posts like "codename 'o' appears on screens" (post) are rumours until the product ships.
  • "Pending approval". Plans that still need a regulator's OK, like SpaceX's phone network (post), can change or never happen.
  • One source, no link. This channel rarely links its sources. Find the original before you repeat a number.

One thing to do today

Pick one task your business repeats every day, like sorting enquiries, summarising calls or pulling details out of invoices. Run 20 real examples through the smallest, cheapest model you can find and check the results by hand. If 18 or more are right, you've probably been overpaying.
