Inside Sales Explained: How the Job Works and How to Prepare

What inside sales really is, from first call to an active customer, with a worked logistics example: discovery, pitch, objections, demo and target maths.

Key takeaways
  • Inside sales means selling remotely, by phone, email, video and demos, and owning the deal from first contact to a customer who actually uses what you sold.
  • Learn the business the way a customer sees it: what they buy, what problem it solves, and how the company earns.
  • Ask before you pitch. Understand the business, put a number on the problem, show only what fits, and agree a next step.
  • Work targets backwards from conversion rates. 250 good conversations can turn into 10 active customers.
  • In the interview, tell true stories with details. Don't present outreach reply rates as closed sales.

I recently prepared for an inside sales interview at a logistics company. The notes turned into a guide that works for any inside sales role, so I've cleaned them up here. Logistics is the worked example throughout. The method is the same if you sell software, education or financial products.

1. Inside sales means owning the deal until the customer uses it

Inside sales is selling without meeting in person. You use calls, email, WhatsApp, video meetings and product demos. It's different from field sales, where you visit customers. In many companies it's also different from an SDR (sales development rep), who only books meetings for someone else to close.

A typical inside sales job description asks you to own the whole chain: find or receive leads, understand the customer's needs, demo the product, close the contract, and work with operations so the customer starts using it. The last step is the one people forget.

2. Learn the business the way a customer sees it

Before the interview, be able to explain in 45 seconds what the company sells and why a customer pays. For a logistics company like Delhivery, it sounds like this: "They move goods from where a business stores them to its customers. That covers online parcels, bigger business shipments, full trucks, warehousing and international shipping. A small business gets to reach customers without running its own delivery operation."

Then learn which service fits which customer. Here's the logistics version (example service page):

Finally, know how the company earns. A logistics company earns its shipping and handling charges. It doesn't earn the value of the goods, or the cash it collects on delivery for the seller. Mixing these up in an interview is a quick way to look unprepared.

Know two or three recent company initiatives and explain each in customer language. For example, Delhivery has a tool that handles failed-delivery follow-ups over WhatsApp and calls (SmartNDR). The customer benefit is fewer orders coming back. It is not "zero failed deliveries", so don't promise that.

3. Map the competition by use case, not by brand

"Who are our competitors?" is a standard question. The strong answer splits it by what the customer is buying:

  • Direct competitors can win the same job. For Indian e-commerce parcels that includes Blue Dart, DTDC, Xpressbees, Ekart and Amazon Shipping. For business freight, it's names like TCI Express and Gati.
  • Indirect competitors meet the need another way: the customer's own delivery fleet, or a marketplace handling delivery for its sellers.
  • Partner-competitors do both. Shipping platforms like Shiprocket let sellers choose between many carriers, including yours. They can bring you volume and take your direct customer at the same time.
Example: a detail that shows homework

Ecom Express used to be a Delhivery competitor; Delhivery bought it in 2025. And FedEx and Aramex compete for some international shipments while partnering with Delhivery on others (shareholder letter). Knowing details like these tells the interviewer you went past the homepage.

4. Ask before you pitch

The most common sales mistake is pitching features before understanding the customer. Use this pattern instead:

Good discovery questions for a logistics customer. Ask a few, listen, and follow the thread. Don't fire all of them at once:

  1. What do you sell, and who decides how it's shipped?
  2. Where do shipments start, and where do most go?
  3. Roughly how many shipments a month, and what's your busiest month?
  4. Typical packed weight and size?
  5. Who ships for you now, and what works well?
  6. What's the biggest problem: pickups, speed, failed deliveries, damage or tracking?
  7. If we fix it, who approves a trial, and when could it start?

5. A pitch, start to finish

Here's a made-up example: an online clothing seller.

Example call

Open: "Hi, I'm calling from [company]. We help businesses ship orders across India. Is now a good time for two quick questions about your deliveries?"

Discover: "How many orders do you ship a month? What's the biggest issue with your current setup?" The customer says 1,500 orders, and too many cash-on-delivery orders come back.

Reflect and quantify: "So the problem is orders coming back and the time your team spends chasing them. Before I suggest anything, can I check your main routes, parcel weights and where the failures happen?"

Show what fits: "Then I can show you just the failed-delivery part of our dashboard, and we can agree a small trial with clear measures: delivery success and total cost."

Close the next step: "If the routes and pricing work for you, can we agree the trial routes, who approves them and a start date?"

Notice what's missing: a list of features, a claim to be the cheapest, and a promise you can't check. Only offer a trial if the company actually allows one.

6. Upsell, expand and cross-sell are different things

Interviewers often ask about growing existing accounts. Be precise:

TermMeaningLogistics example
UpsellMove to a better, pricier version of the same thingUrgent orders move to a faster service
Volume expansionMore use of the same thing300 parcels a month grows to 600 because you win more of their shipping
Cross-sellAdd a different service they also needA parcel customer starts supplying shops and adds part-truckload shipping

One rule: if the customer has an unresolved complaint, fix that first. Pushing a new service on an unhappy customer loses the account.

7. Handle objections without reaching for a discount

Customer saysStrong response
"You're more expensive.""Let's compare the same routes, weights and charges. Is the worry the rate itself, or the total cost once failed deliveries and staff time are included?"
"We already use another company.""What works well today, and where's the gap? We could test one route without changing everything."
"I had a bad experience with you.""Can you walk me through what happened? I'll check it with operations and tell you what would change before asking you to try again."
"Just send me the details.""Sure. What matters most to you: rates on your main routes, or how we handle failed deliveries? And when could we go through it together?"
"Give me a bigger discount.""What volume would that price be based on? I'll check what's approved. I don't want to agree to something we can't keep."
"Not interested."Check once whether it's the fit or the timing. Then respect it and note it accurately.
Never improvise a price

Quote only from the approved rate card, after you have the route, weight, size, volume and payment type. A made-up price to win a call becomes a problem for operations, and for you.

8. Run a demo that answers one question

A good demo isn't a tour of every menu. It answers the customer's question:

  1. Restate their problem and agree what the demo should show.
  2. Show how orders get in: an upload, or a link to their online store.
  3. Show booking, labels and pickup, briefly.
  4. Spend most of the time on their problem. For returns, that's the failed-delivery screen.
  5. Show money and support: payments, cash-on-delivery settlement, who to call.
  6. Ask "Does this solve it?" and agree the next step.

9. Work targets backwards from conversion rates

"How would you hit your target?" The weak answer is "make more calls". The strong answer works backwards:

Spread over 20 working days, that's about 50 attempts and 12–13 real conversations a day. If results fall behind, find the stage where people drop off (connecting, qualifying, closing, or starting to use it) instead of just dialling more.

Customers think in numbers too. Say a seller ships 1,000 orders a month and returns fall from 20% to 15%. That's 50 fewer returns. If each return costs them ₹120, they save ₹6,000 a month, minus any extra fees. That's the kind of maths that closes a trial. Call it an estimate, not a guarantee.

10. The interview itself

Tell true stories, with details

Use Situation, Task, Action, Result, and spend most of the time on what you did. Expect follow-up questions on every number on your resume: who was the customer, what was the objection, what did you personally close, was the money actually paid.

  • Don't present an outreach reply rate as a sales conversion rate.
  • Have one lost deal ready, and what you changed after it.
  • If you've never carried a formal sales target, say so and show real results instead.
  • If you come from a technical background, expect "won't you get bored of calls?" Answer it directly and honestly.

Questions to ask them

  • What does success look like in the first 90 days: signed accounts, active customers, or revenue?
  • What's the mix of incoming leads, outbound prospecting and growing existing accounts?
  • Who owns getting a new customer started, and how does sales work with operations?
  • How are incentives calculated and paid? (Usually a question for HR.)

Words to avoid

"We're always the cheapest." "Zero failed deliveries." "Same-day everywhere." Invented targets. A list of tools when they asked how you'd close a customer.

One thing to do today

Pick the company you're interviewing with. Write its 45-second explanation, three discovery questions and one worked target calculation. Then say them out loud to a friend who interrupts you with "your competitor is cheaper". That one rehearsal is worth more than another hour of reading.
